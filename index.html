Running into challenges facefirst and learning through building

Hello! I'm Sammy Dandu, a second-year graduate student at the University of Maryland who’s pursuing a Master’s in Machine Learning. I’m all about exploring different fields that I find interesting and building tools that enable me to find solutions to the questions or problems I see in each field. Whether it’s building a financial scenario generator that tries to predict how the market will move in the future based on different economic conditions or figuring out whether adding more layers to a quantum circuit makes it more powerful, I try to discover questions by following my interests. I may not know a lot about a topic initially, but as I decide to focus on a question, I hone in on it obsessively, learning everything I can about that topic and then use that understanding to build my own solution to the question I was figuring out. Through this process, I am able to keep pushing myself to expand the boundaries of my knowledge by putting myself into unfamiliar territory first and finding a way to conquer it.

A Note From Me

A little more about me.

Hi,

I used to think learning meant having the right answer ready before anyone asked.

When I was pre-med, my routine was simple: memorize, memorize, memorize, then repeat it all on an exam. I could prepare for questions I knew were coming. But I began to wonder what happened when the problem didn't have an answer key.

That question changed how I learn.

I stopped waiting to feel qualified before getting involved. I joined a robotics team knowing almost nothing about robotics. I stepped into quantum computing research with only a couple of courses behind me. Neither decision was comfortable, and that was the point. I wanted to work with people who knew more than I did and take on problems that would force me to grow.

I still study. I just don't treat studying as the finish line anymore. I build something, find out what I don't understand, ask better questions, and try again.

That approach has changed what I've been able to build. I developed a computer-vision defect detection system using more than 10,000 images and a robotic navigation simulator that passed all nine of its initial validation runs. I also published a research evidence search tool and built a transit tracker using live data. None of those projects began with a perfect roadmap. I learned what I needed as I went.

Of course, not every experiment worked. I went through 42 notebooks on one project before realizing the available data couldn't support the product I wanted to build. I archived it. That was frustrating, but it taught me something just as valuable as a successful launch: knowing when to change course is part of good engineering.

As an early-career engineer and researcher, I believe this mindset will serve me well. I'll put in the work to get up to speed, ask questions when I need to, and volunteer for challenges that push me beyond what I already know.

What do I want in return? The chance to keep learning.

Sounds like a pretty good deal to me.

I like to build, I like to learn, and for some reason I genuinely love to work.

I look forward to hearing from you.

Sammy Dandu
